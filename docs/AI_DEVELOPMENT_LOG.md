# AI-Assisted Development and Prompt Log

**AI for Business Decisions - ESSEC Business School**

## Purpose of this document

This document summarizes the development of the Citi Bike Fleet Availability Planner, from defining the business problem to building, testing and deploying the final application.

The project was completed in the context of Team 9's AI for Business Decisions course assignment at ESSEC Business School. I personally carried out the application's technical development, using ChatGPT extensively to generate and refine code, investigate problems, compare models and implement improvements.

Because development involved a long, iterative conversation with AI, I did not maintain a complete, separately numbered prompt library from the beginning. The examples at the end are selected from actual discussions. This is therefore a retrospective development and AI-usage log rather than an exhaustive transcript.

## 1. DEFINING THE BUSINESS PROBLEM

We began by identifying a recurring operational challenge for bike-sharing operators: ensuring that the right bicycles are available in the right locations at the right times.

Customer journeys continually redistribute bicycles across the city. Consequently, some neighborhoods can experience bicycle shortages while others accumulate surplus bicycles or have insufficient empty docks.

Our objective was to build a decision-support application for an operations manager that could:

1. Analyze historical customer usage and bicycle movements.
2. Predict future departures and returns across the network.
3. Compare predicted activity with available bicycles and docking capacity.
4. Recommend possible bicycle relocations under managerial constraints.

We evaluated different bike-sharing systems, initially considering Chicago's Divvy network before selecting New York City's Citi Bike.

Citi Bike was particularly suitable because both classic and electric bicycles operate within a primarily station-based network, and the operator publishes detailed historical trip data together with live bicycle and docking availability.

The application was conceived as a prototype for a bike-sharing operator using data generated by its own operations, demonstrated using publicly accessible Citi Bike records.

## 2. DATA COLLECTION AND PREPARATION

### Historical trip records

We downloaded twelve months of Citi Bike trip data, covering October 2025 through September 2026. The original dataset contained approximately 45 million recorded trips. Each month's data consisted of multiple CSV files because of the large number of observations.

We excluded files beginning with "JC", which refer to Jersey City rather than the NYC dataset selected for our project.

Using AI-generated and subsequently tested Python scripts, we processed the trip files month by month rather than attempting to load the entire dataset into memory simultaneously.

The preprocessing stage produced compressed summaries of:

- Bicycle departures and returns by station, time and bicycle type.

- Daily usage, including membership categories.

- Trip-duration distributions.

- Data-quality statistics.

This reduced a very large collection of individual trip records into considerably smaller datasets suitable for analysis and forecasting.

### Geographic data

We selected NYC Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (NTAs) as the geographic units of analysis. Using official geographic boundaries and Citi Bike station information, we assigned stations to neighborhoods and created the geographic structure used by both application dashboards. The final forecasting system covers 131 NYC neighborhoods containing mapped stations.

### Weather data

We obtained historical hourly weather observations through Open-Meteo, including temperature, precipitation, wind and other weather conditions. These observations were matched with historical trip data by date and hour. Weather variables were included in model comparison to determine whether they improved prediction accuracy. For future forecasts, the application retrieves weather predictions through an API.

## 3. DATA VALIDATION AND AN IMPORTANT DEBUGGING PROBLEM

One of the biggest technical difficulties arose when connecting historical trip records to the current station directory.

Our first forecasting script reported that 0% of historical departures and returns could be matched to stations. As a result, it produced datasets containing zero trip counts and meaningless model evaluation results.

Through inspection and AI-assisted debugging, we identified the problem: historical trip files and the live station directory sometimes referred to the same stations using different identifier formats. In particular, historical files often used the station's short_name, while the initial matching procedure relied on station_id.

We corrected the matching procedure to recognize both identifiers and rebuilt the forecasting datasets. The corrected process recovered approximately:

- 98.25% of departure events.

- 98.24% of return events.

This was an important lesson: a forecasting algorithm can execute successfully while producing completely invalid results if its input data have not been properly checked. We treated station-matching coverage and data-quality checks as essential prerequisites for training.

## 4. BUILDING AND COMPARING PREDICTIVE MODELS

After preparing the data, we aggregated departures and returns into hourly observations by neighborhood and bicycle type. We trained separate models for departures and returns, since these represent different operational flows.

We compared four forecasting approaches:

| **Approach**                            | **Departure MAE** | **Return MAE** |
|-----------------------------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Historical hourly-average baseline      | 10.554            | 10.511         |
| Poisson regression                      | 10.030            | 10.238         |
| Gradient-boosted trees without weather  | 9.646             | 8.984          |
| **Gradient-boosted trees with weather** | **7.168**         | **6.878**      |

MAE (Mean Absolute Error) measures the average absolute difference between predicted and observed hourly trip counts at the modeled geographic and bicycle-type level.

The comparison used a chronological validation approach, holding out a later historical period for evaluation rather than randomly mixing observations from different dates. Gradient-boosted trees incorporating weather performed best for both departures and returns.

Relative to the historical-average baseline, this approach reduced MAE by approximately 32.1% for departures and 34.6% for returns.

We selected this approach for both forecasting targets and saved the trained models for use by the application. This distinction is important: model training and selection occur before the application is used. The manager does not retrain the models every time a planning scenario changes.

## 5. TRANSLATING PREDICTIONS INTO OPERATIONAL DECISIONS

An important conceptual challenge was determining how predicted departures and returns should translate into actual bicycle requirements.

We could not simply compare predicted daily rentals against available bicycles, because bicycles return throughout the day and can be rented repeatedly.

We therefore developed a separate calculation that:

1. Predicts hourly departures and returns for each neighborhood and bicycle type.
2. Tracks cumulative net bicycle movements over the selected planning period.
3. Determines the maximum predicted depletion of bicycle inventory.
4. Adds a manager-selected safety reserve.
5. Calculates a target starting stock and compares it with current bicycle availability.

The safety reserve is based on a configurable percentage of the busiest forecast day's departures, with a minimum reserve of two bicycles per neighborhood and type.

We also incorporated available docking spaces, since adding bicycles to a neighborhood is not necessarily physically feasible.

The relocation algorithm identifies potential donor neighborhoods with surplus bicycles and proposes transfers under constraints such as:

- Maximum number of bicycles the operator can relocate.

- Maximum straight-line transfer distance.

- Whether movements must remain within the same borough.

- Availability of donor bicycles and receiving dock capacity.

The predictive component uses machine learning; the relocation component is a rule-based allocation procedure, not another trained AI model. The result is a managerial decision-support scenario rather than an automatically executable dispatch plan.

## 6. DEVELOPING THE FIRST APPLICATION

We implemented the application in Python using Streamlit. The first working version contained two dashboards.

### Dashboard 1 - Understand Usage

Allowed managers to explore historical bicycle activity using geographic maps, neighborhood rankings, usage KPIs, hourly patterns, weekday heatmaps and seasonal trends.

### Dashboard 2 - Plan Availability

Combined forecasts, current bicycle availability and docking capacity to identify estimated bicycle needs, display projected inventory trajectories and propose relocation scenarios.

The initial version demonstrated the complete business workflow, but testing revealed several opportunities to make the interface more intuitive.

## 7. ITERATIVE APPLICATION IMPROVEMENTS

Instead of immediately accepting the first working application, we reviewed it from the perspective of an operations manager. We questioned the meaning of the KPIs, tested unusual scenarios and repeatedly modified the interface based on the problems we identified.

### Clarifying inventory targets

We noticed that some neighborhoods could show a positive projected bicycle balance throughout the day while still be classified as needing additional bicycles. The explanation was the safety reserve: a neighborhood might have enough bicycles to avoid reaching zero, but not enough to maintain the desired reserve.

We improved the application by displaying the safety-reserve threshold directly on the projected-stock chart. We also introduced a neighborhood-level inventory summary showing current available bicycles, target starting stock, predicted peak depletion, safety reserve, additional bike need and available receiving capacity.

### Improving geographic analysis

We made neighborhood selection possible through ranking tables and synchronized selections with relevant charts. We separated the map and table views on the planning dashboard, allowing a manager to keep the table ranked by bicycle need while switching the map to show nearby bicycle surpluses. We also distinguished citywide metrics from metrics affected by neighborhood selection.

### Improving relocation controls

The original relocation budget was limited to 500 bicycles. We recognized this as an arbitrary interface restriction rather than an actual operational constraint. We replaced it with a manager-controlled numerical input. We also improved the relocation summary to show the initial estimated need, proposed transfers, remaining need and percentage addressed by the scenario.

### Testing alternative maps

To introduce direct geographic selection, we experimented with different interactive mapping implementations. However, the alternative map had inferior navigation and visual clarity compared with the original Plotly version.

We built a separate small map-testing application to compare alternatives, ultimately deciding to preserve the original map appearance and smooth zooming while retaining neighborhood selection through tables and dropdowns.

### Managing live data

We initially allowed API data to be refreshed when cached information expired during normal application interactions. We subsequently changed this behavior so station-status and weather information are retrieved when a session starts, then retained until the manager explicitly clicks Refresh.

This prevents changing inputs from unexpectedly altering results while a manager evaluates different scenarios. We also made data freshness and fallback conditions more transparent.

## 8. ADDITIONAL HISTORICAL ANALYSIS

Following main application development, we added two analytical features to Dashboard 1.

### Manhattan-outer borough directional flows

We created an hourly chart comparing trips traveling from Manhattan toward the outer boroughs with journeys in the opposite direction. This allows managers to investigate commuting-related directional patterns and helps explain why geographic fleet imbalances can emerge even when overall network usage is high.

### Seasonality and weather

We added descriptive charts illustrating relationships between bicycle usage and temperature, rainfall, snowfall and wind, alongside the existing monthly trend. These charts complement the forecasting model by helping managers understand the historical relationship between ridership and external conditions.

The seasonal and weather analysis uses a fixed historical reference period and is independent of the interactive usage filters. To avoid overcrowding the dashboard, we placed these charts inside an expandable Seasonality & Weather section. The weather relationships are descriptive associations, not proof of causation.

## 9. VERIFICATION, DEPLOYMENT AND FINAL PACKAGING

Throughout development, we preserved earlier application versions rather than overwriting the only working copy. We also tested new functionality and corrected errors before adopting each revised version.

A final controlled comparison was conducted between the first application and the completed version, using the same trained models, historical weather and saved station-status data. Across three tested one-day scenarios, the versions produced identical hourly forecasts, inventory requirements, dock-risk calculations and relocation recommendations.

The final version intentionally uses an improved safety-buffer calculation for multi-day planning, so multi-day inventory targets differ from the original implementation.

The application was also deployed through GitHub and Streamlit Community Cloud, making it accessible through a web browser.

Finally, we created a compact standalone package containing the application code, trained models, processed data and installation instructions. The original raw trip files and preprocessing scripts were excluded from this final runtime package because they are not required to run the trained application.

## 11. ROLE OF GENERATIVE AI AND HUMAN SUPERVISION

Generative AI was used as a development assistant throughout the project, primarily for:

- Structuring the problem and designing the application workflow.

- Planning data collection and processing.

- Generating Python scripts for data preparation and model training.

- Investigating programming errors and data-quality issues.

- Comparing forecasting approaches and interpreting their outputs.

- Building and refining the Streamlit application.

- Explaining calculations and challenging operational assumptions.

- Supporting application testing, documentation and deployment.

My role was to define the application's technical objectives, obtain and validate the data, execute and test AI-generated code, critically evaluate model results, identify inconsistencies, determine the application's functionality, and make the final implementation and design decisions.

Several important improvements resulted from challenging AI-generated solutions rather than accepting them immediately.

Generative AI was therefore part of the development process, while the deployed application itself uses trained predictive models and rule-based relocation calculations. It does not contain a conversational generative-AI agent.

## 12. REMAINING LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENT

The final application is a working academic decision-support prototype, not a production fleet-management system.

Important limitations include:

- Historical trip records capture completed rides, not unsuccessful rental attempts or all unmet demand.

- Departure and return forecasts are produced independently and are not forced to balance at network level.

- Current station availability is used as hypothetical starting inventory for a future planning period.

- Relocation recommendations operate at neighborhood level; individual station availability and actual vehicle routes require further verification.

- Live APIs may occasionally fail, requiring saved station snapshots or historical-weather fallback data.

A production implementation would require continued model validation, monitoring, more detailed station-level allocation, route optimization and operational integration.

The project's data infrastructure supports future maintenance: Citi Bike publishes recurring trip histories, and live station information and weather services provide additional inputs. Models could be periodically retrained and evaluated as newer operational data become available.

The project illustrates that the principal challenge in applying AI to business decisions is not simply training a predictive model, but ensuring that its outputs are based on valid data, interpreted correctly and translated into useful decisions under real operational constraints.
